Competitor pricing is useful context, but it is a weak compass. It tells you what other companies charge, not what your buyer values, what pain you remove, or what package will survive a real sales conversation.

Early pricing should teach you. It should reveal whether the buyer, problem, and promise are strong enough to support the business you want to build.

## Start with the cost of the problem

Before choosing a number, understand the cost of doing nothing.

Ask:

- How much time does the current workaround waste?
- What mistakes happen because the workflow is broken?
- Who is affected when the problem is not solved?
- Does the problem create revenue loss, risk, delay, or team drag?
- Is this pain visible to the budget owner?

If the problem has no clear cost, pricing will feel arbitrary.

## Define the buyer and the package together

Price depends on who is buying and what they believe they are getting.

A founder, recruiter, finance lead, and operator may all value the same tool differently. A self-serve tool, done-with-you setup, operator pass, and enterprise workflow are different packages even if the software is similar.

Write the package in one sentence:

`For [buyer], this helps [job] by removing [pain] and producing [outcome].`

If that sentence is fuzzy, the price will be fuzzy too.

## Pick a first price that creates a real signal

Free users can teach you about usage. Paying users teach you about value.

A useful first price should be:

- high enough that the buyer has to care
- low enough that you can sell without months of procurement
- simple enough to explain in one sentence
- tied to a package, not just access

The goal is not perfection. The goal is a price that creates learning.

## Do not confuse discounting with validation

Discounts can help close early design partners, but they can also hide weak demand.

If every buyer only says yes after a heavy discount, ask:

- Is the pain not urgent enough?
- Is the buyer wrong?
- Is the package unclear?
- Is the proof too thin?
- Is the price fine but the sales process weak?

Discounting should be a deliberate experiment, not a reflex.

## Test willingness to pay in conversation

Do not ask, "Would you pay for this?" Most people will answer politely.

Ask better questions:

- What budget would this come from?
- What would you compare this against?
- Who would need to approve it?
- At what price would this become a serious decision?
- What result would make renewal obvious?

These questions reveal the buying reality behind the compliment.

## Revisit pricing as the product learns

Your first price is not a constitution.

Change it when:

- the buyer becomes clearer
- the use case narrows
- proof gets stronger
- support cost changes
- the package shifts from tool to outcome

Pricing should mature with the product.

## The real goal

Do not copy a competitor and hope the market forgives the guess.

Use price as a way to learn what buyers value, how urgently they want the problem solved, and whether your package makes the value easy to understand.
