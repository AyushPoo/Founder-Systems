Startup document packets often arrive as a pile: deck, memo, spreadsheet, contract, notes, screenshots, maybe a few files with names like "final_final_v3." The work is not just summarizing them. The work is finding the claims that matter and checking whether the files support them.

This matters because polished documents can hide weak proof. Messy documents can contain the truth. You need a review method that does not get fooled by either.

## Separate claims from evidence

Start by listing the claims the packet is asking you to believe.

Examples:

- revenue is growing
- churn is under control
- the team understands the buyer
- the financing terms are standard
- the product has strong engagement
- the market is ready now

Then ask where each claim is supported. Is it in a spreadsheet, customer quote, contract, deck slide, or founder note? If you cannot find the source, mark it as unsupported.

## Check dates before conclusions

Stale evidence is one of the easiest things to miss.

A deck may say the company has 40 active customers, while the sheet shows 29 last month. A memo may cite a pilot that ended six months ago. A cap table may not match a newer financing doc.

For each important number, capture:

- date
- source file
- owner
- whether a newer source contradicts it

The newest file is not always the most accurate, but date order helps you find the right question.

## Look for contradictions across files

Contradictions are where the useful work begins.

Common examples:

- deck says enterprise, customer list says mostly small business
- memo says efficient growth, model shows rising acquisition cost
- update says hiring is paused, budget assumes new headcount
- SAFE note implies one valuation cap, summary says another

Do not smooth these over. Write the contradiction plainly and ask what would resolve it.

## Watch for missing proof

Some packets are not wrong. They are incomplete.

Red flags include:

- big market claims without buyer evidence
- revenue growth without retention or cohort context
- product claims without usage or workflow data
- hiring plans without budget impact
- financing summaries without actual terms

Missing proof is not an accusation. It is a request for the file or context needed to make a decision safely.

## Match the lens to the document type

Different files deserve different questions.

For decks, ask whether the narrative earns belief slide by slide. For memos, ask whether the reasoning is explicit. For spreadsheets, ask which assumptions drive the output. For financing docs, ask what obligations, discounts, caps, or control terms matter.

Do not use one generic summary lens for everything. A founder deck and a SAFE are not the same kind of document.

## End with decisions, not trivia

A document review can drown in details. The useful output should answer:

- What did we learn?
- What is well supported?
- What is contradicted?
- What is missing?
- What decision changes because of this packet?

If nothing changes, the review is probably too shallow or the documents are not decision-ready.

## The real goal

Good document intelligence does not make every file feel tidy. It makes the important uncertainty visible.

That is what keeps founders, operators, and investors from trusting the wrong version of the story.
